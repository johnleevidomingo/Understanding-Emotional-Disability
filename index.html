Part 1 · Definition and overview

What is Emotional Disturbance?

The IDEA definition

Under federal law, emotional disturbance is a condition showing one or more of these characteristics over a long period of time and to a marked degree that adversely affects a child's educational performance (34 C.F.R. § 300.8(c)(4)):

  1. An inability to learn that cannot be explained by intellectual, sensory, or health factors
  2. An inability to build or maintain satisfactory relationships with peers and teachers
  3. Inappropriate types of behavior or feelings under normal circumstances
  4. A general, pervasive mood of unhappiness or depression
  5. A tendency to develop physical symptoms or fears associated with personal or school problems

ED is an education category, not a single diagnosis. It is an umbrella that can include students with anxiety disorders, depression, bipolar disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, PTSD, and other mental health conditions, as long as the condition meets the criteria above and affects learning (Undivided, 2025).

What the law includes and excludes. The definition explicitly includes schizophrenia. It does not apply to students who are "socially maladjusted" unless they also meet the criteria for emotional disturbance (34 C.F.R. § 300.8(c)(4)(ii)).

Effects reach beyond emotions. Depending on the condition, a student's physical, social, or cognitive skills can also be affected (CEC Division for Emotional & Behavioral Health, n.d.).

A note on the name. Many advocates say "emotional disturbance" is stigmatizing. Some states now use emotional disability instead, with the same definition. New York made the switch in 2022 (NYSED, 2022), and California followed in 2024. This site uses "ED" for both.

Part 2 · Characteristics and impact on learning

What ED can look like in a classroom

Students with ED don't all look alike. A helpful way to sort what teachers see is externalizing behavior, which is directed outward and easy to notice, and internalizing behavior, which is directed inward and easy to miss. Many students show both.

Externalizing

The student everyone notices

  • Refusing tasks or arguing with adults
  • Verbal outbursts, yelling, or swearing
  • Leaving the seat or room without permission
  • Physical aggression toward property or people
  • Reacting intensely to small frustrations or corrections
Internalizing

The student who gets overlooked

  • Withdrawing, rarely speaking, avoiding group work
  • Frequent stomachaches, headaches, or nurse visits
  • Persistent sadness, flat mood, or irritability
  • Excessive worry, perfectionism, or fear of mistakes
  • Skipping school or avoiding certain classes

How it affects learning

AreaWhat can happen
AcademicsMissed instruction from removals, absences, or time spent dysregulated leads to gaps in skills, even when the student's ability is average or higher.
Attention and effortWorry, low mood, or stress makes it hard to concentrate, start tasks, and persist through challenging work.
RelationshipsConflict or withdrawal makes partner and group work difficult, and strained relationships with teachers can make students less willing to ask for help.
CommunicationStudents may not have the words for what they feel, so distress comes out as behavior instead.
Access and placementStudents with ED are more likely than other students with disabilities to be served in separate schools (CEC, n.d.), which can limit time with general education peers.
Long-term outcomesStudents with ED have had the highest dropout rate of any IDEA category (Champ Morera, 2026), so engagement and belonging at school matter.
Strengths

Strengths to build on

A strengths-based approach sees the whole student, not just the behavior. Every student is different, but these strengths often show up in students with ED once they feel safe.

Resilience

Many have worked through hard experiences and keep showing up. That persistence can be directed toward goals.

Emotional perceptiveness

Students who are highly sensitive to emotion often notice how others feel before anyone else does.

Loyalty and honesty

Once trust is built, these students are often fiercely loyal and very direct about what's working and what isn't.

Creativity

Art, music, writing, building, and games can be powerful outlets and real areas of talent.

Passion for interests

Strong interests can be used as a hook for engagement, leadership roles, and motivation.

Self-advocacy potential

Students who learn to name their feelings and needs can become strong advocates for themselves and for others.

Common misconceptions

Myths vs. facts

Myth

"They're just choosing to misbehave."

Fact

ED is a disability. Behavior usually serves a purpose, like escaping a task that feels impossible or getting connection. A functional behavioral assessment helps find that purpose so the team can teach a better way to meet the need (IRIS Center, 2025).

Myth

"Students with ED are violent."

Fact

Many students with ED are quiet, anxious, or depressed. Their struggles are internal, and they are often the students teachers miss.

Myth

"It's caused by bad parenting."

Fact

There is no single known cause. Biology, environment, and life experiences can all play a role (CEC Division for Emotional & Behavioral Health, n.d.). Blaming families damages the partnership a student needs most.

Myth

"Stricter punishment will fix it."

Fact

Removals cost students instruction and rarely teach a new skill. Proactive strategies such as behavior-specific praise and Check-In/Check-Out have research showing they reduce problem behavior (Royer et al., 2019; Maggin et al., 2015).

Myth

"These students belong in a separate classroom."

Fact

IDEA requires the least restrictive environment. With the right supports, many students with ED learn successfully alongside their peers.

Part 3 · Evidence-based supports and accommodations

Supports that work

Schools often organize behavior support in three tiers, as in Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS). Strong classroom practices help everyone, and support gets more intensive and individualized for students who need more.

Tier 1

Every student, every classroom

Behavior-specific praise

Name exactly what the student did well: "You started your warm-up right away. That's focus." A systematic review found it increased time on task and reduced inappropriate behavior, and classified it as a potentially evidence-based practice (Royer et al., 2019).

Precorrection and predictable routines

Remind students of the expectation before a known trigger, such as a transition or group work, and keep routines consistent so school feels predictable and safe (Riggs & Landrum, 2023).

Instructional choice

Offer two acceptable options, like "pencil or laptop" or "problems 1–5 or 6–10." Choice gives students a sense of control without lowering expectations (Royer et al., 2019).

Tier 2

Targeted support for some students

Check-In/Check-Out (CICO)

The student checks in with a mentor each morning, gets feedback on a daily point card from each teacher, and checks out at the end of the day. A systematic review supports its use for reducing challenging behavior (Maggin et al., 2015).

Social skills instruction

Explicitly teach and practice skills like joining a group, handling disagreement, and asking for help, the same way you would teach an academic skill (Yell et al., 2013).

Self-monitoring

Students track their own behavior, like "Was I on task?", on a simple chart. This builds self-awareness and independence.

Tier 3

Individualized, intensive support

Functional behavioral assessment and behavior intervention plan (FBA/BIP)

The team gathers data on what happens before and after a behavior to figure out its function. They then write a plan that teaches a replacement behavior serving the same need (IRIS Center, 2025).

Cognitive behavioral interventions

Teach students to notice their thoughts, feelings, and body signals, and use coping strategies such as self-talk, problem solving, and calming routines (Yell et al., 2013).

Trauma-informed, team-based care

Many students with ED have experienced trauma. Combining PBIS with trauma-informed care means focusing on safety, relationships, and regulation before consequences (Riggs & Landrum, 2023). School counselors, psychologists, and families work together.

Common accommodations (IEP or 504 plan)

  • Access to a calm-down space or scheduled breaks
  • A nonverbal signal to request a break
  • Daily check-in with a trusted adult
  • Advance warning before transitions or schedule changes
  • Assignments broken into smaller chunks
  • Preferential seating away from high-traffic areas
  • Testing in a separate, quieter setting
  • Flexible deadlines during periods of crisis
  • Private, not public, redirection and feedback
  • A written behavior plan shared with every teacher
What it may look like in a classroom

Same moment, two responses

The moment: It's 10:15 in a 7th-grade English class. Students are starting a timed writing prompt. Kai, who has an IEP under ED for anxiety and depression, puts his head down, pushes his paper away, and mutters, "This is stupid. I'm not doing it."

Kai is a fictional student created for this example.

Response that escalates

  1. From the front of the room: "Kai, head up. Everyone else is working."
  2. Kai doesn't move. "If you don't start, you'll lose recess."
  3. Kai says something rude. The teacher writes a referral and sends him to the office.

Result: Kai avoids the task he feared, which teaches him that refusing works. He misses instruction and feels embarrassed in front of peers. His trust in the teacher drops.

Response that supports

  1. The teacher walks over and kneels beside Kai, speaking quietly: "Looks like this one feels like a lot."
  2. Offers a choice: "Want to talk your first sentence out with me, or start with a bulleted list instead?"
  3. Kai writes two bullets. The teacher gives specific praise: "You got your ideas down. That's the hardest part."
  4. Later, she notes the pattern of refusal during timed writing for Kai's IEP team.

Result: Kai stays in class and starts the task. The teacher collects useful data about a trigger, and the relationship gets stronger.

Debrief: The second teacher read the behavior as communication. Kai's refusal was likely about escaping a task that felt overwhelming. She stayed calm and private, lowered the barrier to starting with a choice, reinforced the effort right away, and used the moment as data for an FBA.

Resources

For families and educators

For families

Partnering with your child's school

  • You can request a special education evaluation in writing at any time if you think your child may need support.
  • You are an equal member of the IEP team. Share what works at home and what triggers your child.
  • Ask whether an FBA has been done and request a copy of any behavior plan.
  • Ask how the school will communicate positives, not just problems.
  • Connect with community mental health supports and parent groups. Hawaii families can contact the Leadership in Disabilities & Achievement of Hawaii (LDAH) parent center for free help with special education.
For educators

Where to learn more

  • IRIS Center: Functional Behavioral Assessment module, free and self-paced
  • Center on PBIS for tiered supports, CICO guides, and classroom practices
  • CEC Division for Emotional & Behavioral Health for research and practice briefs
  • Read each student's IEP and BIP before the first day, and ask the case manager what has worked before.
  • Keep simple notes on when behavior happens and what comes before it. That data drives good plans.
Part 4 · What teachers should know
If there's one thing every teacher should know about this disability, it is…
Behavior is communication, and the relationship is the intervention.

When a student with ED shuts down or blows up, the behavior is telling you something: this feels too hard, I'm scared, I need to feel in control, I need someone to notice me. Punishing the behavior without hearing the message rarely changes anything. Every strategy on this site, from specific praise to an FBA, works better when a student believes their teacher is on their side. The most effective thing a teacher can do is stay calm, stay curious, and keep showing up for that student, especially on the hard days.
